# The Work of Becoming Oracle — Cards

This file defines the planned **60-card** sequence for The Work of Becoming Oracle.

Each card may describe a literal job, professional role, project stage, business condition, workplace dynamic, skill-development need, ethical question, sustainability issue, or career transition. Work is treated as an exchange of labor, value, responsibility, identity, and consequence rather than a measure of human worth.

Current salary, law, benefits, market conditions, contracts, deadlines, and safety requirements must be verified through real-world sources rather than divination.

These short descriptions are planning summaries, not substitutes for future image-led card files. No individual artwork or card-specific `.md` files are included yet.

| Number | Card name | Short description |
|---:|---|---|
| 1 | **The Calling** | A direction feels deeply meaningful and identity-shaping, though purpose still needs practical structure, skill, and sustainable support. |
| 2 | **The Job** | Work is presently an exchange of labor for stability or income and need not carry the burden of being a total life purpose. |
| 3 | **The First Day** | A new role, project, workplace, or professional identity begins through observation, orientation, and manageable first responsibilities. |
| 4 | **The Apprentice** | Growth requires practice, feedback, humility, supervision, and permission to be unfinished while developing real competence. |
| 5 | **The Specialist** | Focused expertise creates value and authority, but may narrow perspective or create dependence on one domain. |
| 6 | **The Generalist** | Breadth, translation, adaptability, and connection across disciplines are central strengths even when harder to summarize in one title. |
| 7 | **The Builder** | Practical creation, systems, maintenance, and durable results matter more than appearance or early praise. |
| 8 | **The Visionary** | A compelling future or original idea directs work, but execution, listening, and shared reality determine whether others can build it. |
| 9 | **The Operator** | Daily coordination, process, reliability, logistics, and follow-through keep the project functioning beyond inspiration. |
| 10 | **The Leader** | Direction, decision, protection, accountability, and use of power become more important than title alone. |
| 11 | **The Collaborator** | Shared creation benefits from complementary skill, communication, credit, boundaries, and clearly divided responsibility. |
| 12 | **The Independent Worker** | Autonomy and authorship are strong, while isolation, sales, administration, and self-management also require attention. |
| 13 | **The Unfinished Prototype** | The idea has a material form ready for testing, learning, criticism, and revision rather than premature perfection. |
| 14 | **The First Client** | External trust and exchange begin; expectations, scope, delivery, payment, and communication need explicit foundations. |
| 15 | **The Difficult Client** | Conflicting expectations, unclear scope, disrespect, dependency, or poor fit tests boundaries and professional process. |
| 16 | **The Supportive Mentor** | Experienced guidance, sponsorship, feedback, and introduction accelerate growth when power remains accountable and non-possessive. |
| 17 | **The Competitive Coworker** | Comparison, scarce recognition, overlapping ambition, or strategic rivalry influences the work environment. |
| 18 | **The Reliable Team** | Trust is built through competence, communication, mutual coverage, credit, and consistent responsibility across people. |
| 19 | **The Broken Team** | Misaligned goals, missing trust, unequal labor, poor leadership, or unresolved conflict prevents collective capacity from functioning. |
| 20 | **The Promotion** | Recognition and expanded access arrive together with greater scrutiny, responsibility, politics, and need for new skill. |
| 21 | **The New Responsibility** | A larger task, dependent person, budget, system, or decision now requires ownership beyond symbolic enthusiasm. |
| 22 | **The Glass Ceiling** | Structural bias, inaccessible networks, policy, status, or hidden criteria limit advancement despite capability and effort. |
| 23 | **The Open Door** | A real opportunity, introduction, vacancy, invitation, or route to advancement is available for timely exploration. |
| 24 | **The Closed Office** | A role, organization, department, or opportunity is unavailable, ending, inaccessible, or no longer worth waiting outside. |
| 25 | **The Interview** | Mutual evaluation is occurring; the worker should assess the organization as carefully as the organization assesses them. |
| 26 | **The Offer** | A concrete opportunity becomes available and should be evaluated through pay, scope, values, conditions, growth, and alternatives. |
| 27 | **The Counteroffer** | Negotiation can revise compensation, role, timing, or commitment, but may not repair the reasons someone wanted to leave. |
| 28 | **The Contract** | Responsibilities, rights, payment, ownership, duration, and exit conditions need explicit agreement and informed review. |
| 29 | **The Hidden Clause** | An overlooked expectation, restriction, cost, ownership term, culture norm, or duty may materially change the apparent opportunity. |
| 30 | **The Paid Skill** | Developed capacity has recognized market or organizational value and can support income, bargaining, or specialization. |
| 31 | **The Unpaid Labor** | Emotional, domestic, administrative, creative, or preparatory work is being expected without fair recognition, compensation, or consent. |
| 32 | **The Side Project** | A secondary effort offers experimentation, income, identity, or future transition while competing with limited energy and time. |
| 33 | **The Shared Venture** | Joint ownership or entrepreneurship requires explicit contribution, equity, authority, risk, conflict process, and exit planning. |
| 34 | **The Founder** | Initiation, ownership, risk, culture, and responsibility concentrate around the person creating the organization or product. |
| 35 | **The Second Career** | Earlier skill and experience are being translated into a new field, identity, pace, or form of contribution. |
| 36 | **The Necessary Training** | Advancement depends on learning, practice, supervised experience, accessibility, or foundational knowledge not yet complete. |
| 37 | **The Certification** | Formal proof, license, assessment, credential, or institutional recognition controls access despite not representing the whole skill. |
| 38 | **The Portfolio** | Evidence of work, process, range, and result communicates capability more reliably than aspiration or title alone. |
| 39 | **The Public Recognition** | Visibility, praise, award, audience, or credit increases opportunity while also creating pressure and expectation. |
| 40 | **The Invisible Contribution** | Essential labor supports the outcome without adequate visibility, authorship, authority, or reward. |
| 41 | **The Deadline** | Time pressure requires prioritization and realistic scope; urgency should not automatically justify unsafe or exploitative work. |
| 42 | **The Bottleneck** | One resource, person, approval, dependency, or process limits the speed of the entire system. |
| 43 | **The Rework** | Earlier output must be corrected, rebuilt, clarified, or adapted; learning matters more than defending sunk effort. |
| 44 | **The Breakthrough** | Preparation and persistence overcome a barrier, opening a new level of skill, product viability, recognition, or access. |
| 45 | **The Pivot** | Evidence shows that goal, audience, method, role, or business model should change while preserving useful learning. |
| 46 | **The Layoff** | Employment ends through organizational decision, restructuring, scarcity, or policy; the loss is real without defining personal worth. |
| 47 | **The Resignation** | The worker chooses to leave a role, condition, mission, or identity and must plan for consequence, transition, and recovery. |
| 48 | **The Burned-Out Lantern** | Energy, meaning, focus, or care has been consumed faster than it can be replenished, requiring more than motivational pressure. |
| 49 | **The Rest Cycle** | Recovery, leave, maintenance, reduced output, and restoration are part of sustainable work rather than an interruption of value. |
| 50 | **The Return to Craft** | Attention shifts from politics, scale, status, or exhaustion back to the actual skill and meaningful process of making. |
| 51 | **The Ethical Line** | A professional boundary, safety duty, value, truth, or refusal cannot responsibly be crossed for convenience or reward. |
| 52 | **The Compromise** | Practical accommodation may preserve progress, but should not normalize exploitation, dishonesty, or abandonment of core standards. |
| 53 | **The Mission Drift** | Work has moved away from its stated purpose because of money, status, habit, bureaucracy, audience, or survival pressure. |
| 54 | **The Work That Outgrows You** | A project or organization now requires skill, structure, leadership, or scale beyond one person's current role. |
| 55 | **The Work You Outgrow** | A once-useful role no longer matches capacity, values, learning needs, pay, or the future being built. |
| 56 | **The Sustainable Pace** | Output, rest, compensation, support, body, and responsibility are balanced well enough to continue without predictable collapse. |
| 57 | **The Abundant Season** | Demand, resources, recognition, ideas, or revenue are strong; storage, boundaries, investment, and capacity matter. |
| 58 | **The Lean Season** | Reduced work or resources call for adaptation, prioritization, support, learning, and realistic planning rather than shame. |
| 59 | **The Legacy Project** | Work is being shaped for impact beyond the immediate role, audience, career stage, organization, or lifetime. |
| 60 | **The Work of Becoming** | The central outcome is not only what is produced but the skill, values, relationships, and identity created through the process. |
